# Research synthesis: imagery, layout, generated art, personas & the register

**Method:** Secondary research (peer-reviewed marketing and consumer-psychology papers, plus practitioner sources), applied to OKC Onsite Detailing’s real portfolio. No primary interviews. | **Date:** 2026-11-23 | **Applied in:** v2.2

## Executive summary
The people who buy this work fall into two groups. The most valuable clients (quiet old money, aircraft owners, collectors) respond to **understatement, precision and visible craft**. Loud claims, flashy branding and jargon work against them. The site now speaks to them through **settings** (where their machines live) rather than labels for who they are. It uses a **register** of short, evocative house names, always paired with the plain term. Photographs stay real and in full colour as proof of work. Black-and-white is used only for atmosphere, and generated art is kept strictly abstract.

## Themes

### 1. Quiet signals for the most valuable clients
Han, Nunes & Drèze (2010, *J. Marketing*) sort luxury buyers by wealth and need for status:
- **Patricians** (wealthy, low status-need) prefer quiet branding and pay more for it.
- **Parvenus** (wealthy, high status-need) want loud signals.

Berger & Ward (2010, *JCR*) found that insiders prefer subtle markers only connoisseurs can decode, and that across product categories the most expensive items carry the least visible branding.

**Applied:**
- Restrained palette; the logo is not repeated as decoration.
- A typographic specification sheet with precise material names (LLumar Platinum Bond, Geoshield C2, Undrdog) as insider cues.

### 2. Simplicity and darkness read as premium
- Ton et al. (2023, *J. Marketing*): simple design raises willingness to pay. This is packaging research, applied here by analogy.
- An Emerald study of luxury homepages found darker backgrounds, full-screen imagery and sparse navigation.
- A small study (BI thesis, n=145) found that black-and-white raised perceived luxury through perceived expertise.

**Applied:**
- Nav slimmed to Services · Settings · Care Atlas · Request.
- Full-bleed setting panels on the home page.
- Black-and-white "editorial" treatment for atmosphere only.

### 3. Visible craft adds value
The handmade effect (Fuchs, Schreier & van Osselaer 2015, *J. Marketing*): products made by people are valued more, partly because buyers infer care.

**Applied:** a **By Hand** sequence showing the business's own technicians at work, unretouched.

### 4. Generated art must not depict the objects
Disclosed AI imagery loses perceived authenticity (about −0.27 SD in a pre-registered study; Columbia's "AI in disguise" found the same direction). For a business whose product *is* the appearance of real objects, generated pictures of vehicles would undermine trust.

**Applied:**
- Every object shown is a real photograph.
- Generated art is abstract or diagrammatic and labelled: gloss field, beading, marred vs corrected paint, contour study, plus five new signature pieces.
  - Cool Glass: infrared lines stopped at a pane.
  - Second Skin: impact ripples dissipating.
  - Waterline: wave-interference pattern.
  - Flight Line: streamlines around an airfoil section.
  - Large Format: a modular scale grid.

### 5. Novel language, with guard rails
- Miller & Kahn (2005, *JCR*): ambiguous, evocative names raise preference for **sensory** products, but only when people have attention to spare. The authors expect it to fail in high-stakes categories.
- Song & Schwarz (2009): hard-to-pronounce names read as **risky** (replication contested).
- Brown, Anicich & Galinsky (2020): jargon signals *low* status.

**Applied (full re-terming):**
- House names are 1–3 common English words, and the plain term is always rendered beside them, including in every `<h1>`, all page titles and meta descriptions.
- Forms and the mailto request use **plain** terms, because people filling in a form have no spare attention.
- Banned-word and pronounceability tests guard the register.

### 6. Show their world, not adjectives
Self-congruity effects are robust (r ≈ .31; Aguirre-Rodriguez, Bosnjak & Sirgy 2012). In luxury specifically, imagery of **users and usage** matters more than claims about brand personality (Liu et al. 2012).

**Applied:** five **settings** (hangar, estate, collection, lake, fleet), each shown through its place and objects. Persona labels are never visible (enforced by test).

### 7. The person reading is often not the person paying
Household managers, chief pilots, assistants and family offices handle vendor contact and approve smaller bills (BofA Private Bank; ThinkAdvisor; practitioner sources).

**Applied:** a printable one-page brief at `/for-managers/`. It shows only owner-confirmed commitments.

## How the experience lands with each personality
- **Patrician / estate:** quiet monochrome atmosphere, discretion and one trusted team. Nothing shouts.
- **Aviator:** schedule and access first, chemistry second, adjectives never.
- **Collector:** specification sheets, step counts and unretouched proof.
- **Lake host:** seasonal timing, readiness to host, colour.
- **Fleet principal:** consistency, scale and scheduling.
- **Delegate:** one printable page that answers the questions they get asked.

## Open questions
- Test the register: run an A/B test of house-name-first vs plain-first headings on the service pages, measuring quote starts.
- Interview five to eight current clients per setting to validate the persona table.
- The black-and-white luxury effect rests on one small study. Watch engagement on the setting panels.
